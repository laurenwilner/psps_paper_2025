\section{Introduction}
Climate change has accelerated, driving temperatures higher and increasing variability in precipitation patterns. These higher temperatures and prolonged droughts increase the risk of wildfires. Simultaneously, the number of people living in areas with high wildfire risk has grown.\citep{burke2021changing, radeloff2018rapid} Coupled with fire management practices that yield more available wildfire fuel,\citep{burke2021changing} these factors have led to increased wildfire frequency, severity, and population exposure in the Western United States.\citep{casey2024measuring, radeloff2018rapid, burke2021changing} 

Wildfires impact human health and communities through direct damage and cascading consequences.\citep{casey2024measuring} Residential proximity to wildfires and the fine particulate matter (\PM) from wildfire smoke have been associated with adverse cardiorespiratory, neurological, and mental health outcomes.\citep{mcbrien_wildfire_2023, gould2024health, wang2025differential} Community-level consequences of wildfires include population displacement, damage and destruction of homes and infrastructure, and, importantly, power outages.\citep{hamideh2022wildfire, gordon2025wildfire}

Alone, power outages are associated with elevated healthcare utilization for injuries, hypothermia, hyperthermia, cardiorespiratory outcomes, and mortality likely due to stress, lack of indoor thermal control, reduced emergency and other communication, interrupted medical care, and loss of power to electricity-dependent durable medical equipment (eDME).\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate} When the power goes out, people cannot run air filtration systems, refrigerators, air conditioners, or other electricity-dependent devices.\citep{casey2020power} This can reduce indoor air quality, threaten indoor climate control, and cause stress that may precipitate adverse health outcomes. At particular risk are those reliant on eDME, as back-up batteries generally last less than 12 hours.\citep{molinari2017s} Oxygen concentrators, a device commonly used by those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), often have a battery life of 3-4 hours.\citep{molinari2017s} A narrative review from Casey at al. found that failure of electricity-dependent medical devices, such as ventilators or other respiratory devices, were responsible for many acute respiratory and cardiovascular visits across several different power outage studies.\citep{casey2020power}

Failing electric infrastructure has caused several of California's most devastating wildfires. Indeed, the co-occurrence of wildfires with power outages in California increased from 2018--2020.\citep{do2025spatiotemporal}  Utility infrastructure may ignite wildfires when power lines come into contact with trees or dry ground--typically as a result of high winds, like as California's strong Santa Ana and Diablo winds.\citep{khan2022probabilistic} Wildfires caused by electrical lines can be among the most destructive,\citep{harding2025a} especially when these electrical lines serve dense population centers. 

States have begun to adopt adaptive measures to protect public safety from wildfires caused by electric infrastructure. One such measure, implemented in California beginning in 2012 and increasingly in other states, is Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS) events.\citep{cpuc_psps_2025, PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS} During a PSPS event, electric utilities proactively de-energize power lines to prevent them from causing or worsening wildfires.\citep{cpuc_psps_2025} No studies have evaluated the healthcare utilization impacts of PSPS events, which can result in large populations losing power for over 24 hours. While PSPS events are planned, they may impact health similarly to unplanned outages. 

PSPS events may also pose an amplified threat when wildfires still occur despite the shutoff, exposing individuals to the adverse impacts of losing power and wildfire smoke exposure. This may happen if a PSPS event is (1) used to prevent a wildfire from worsening, or (2) initiated to attempt to reduce already high wildfire risk, but a wildfire still occurs nearby. The hazard of co-occurring power outages and high concentrations of \PM may have synergistic health impacts that exceed the sum of their individual effects. 

To our knowledge, no epidemiological studies have evaluated this co-exposure. Those living with COPD, who often rely on oxygen concentrators, may face disproportionate risks during power outages and exceptional risk when wildfire smoke is also present. 

This study aimed to assess the association between PSPS events, wildfire \PM exposure, and their co-occurrence and respiratory, COPD, cardiovascular, and psychiatric acute healthcare utilization in California from 2013--2019.
