\section{Introduction}
Climate change has accelerated at an unprecedented rate, driving temperatures higher and increasing variability in precipitation patterns. These higher temperatures and prolonged droughts increase the risk of wildfires. Simultaneously, the number of people living in areas with high wildfire risk has grown.\citep{burke2021changing, radeloff2018rapid} Coupled with fire management practices that yield more available wildfire fuel,\citep{burke2021changing} these factors have led to increased wildfire frequency, severity, and population exposure in the Western United States.\citep{casey2024measuring, radeloff2018rapid, burke2021changing} 

Wildfires impact human health and communities through direct damages and cascading consequences.\citep{casey2024measuring} Residential proximity to wildfires and the fine particulate (PM$_{2.5}$) from wildfire smoke have been associated with adverse cardiorespiratory, neurological, and mental health outcomes.\citep{mcbrien_wildfire_2023, gould2024health} Community-level consequences of wildfires include population displacement, damage and destruction of homes and infrastructure, and power outages.\citep{hamideh2022wildfire, gordon2025wildfire}

Several of the most devastating wildfire events in California have been \textit{caused by} electric utility infrastructure. Utility infrastructure may ignite wildfires when electrical infrastructure (e.g., power lines) comes into contact with trees, branches, or dry ground--typically as a result of high winds.\citep{khan2022probabilistic} In California, strong Santa Ana and Diablo winds can bring down power lines, fuel flames, and spread of fire. Wildfires caused by electrical lines can be among the most destructive,\citep{harding2025a} especially because electrical lines serve population centers where the fires may therefore affect more people. 

As wildfires increase in frequency, their co-occurrence with power outages in California has increased.\citep{do2025spatiotemporal}  Given the population impacts of these events individually, coupled with their unknown combined effects, states have adopted adaptive measures to protect public safety. One such measure, implemented in California beginning in 2012 and increasingly adopted by other states, is Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS) events.\citep{cpuc_psps_2025, pse_healthy_energy} During a PSPS event, electric utilities proactively de-energize power lines to prevent them from causing or worsening wildfires.\citep{cpuc_psps_2025} No studies have evaluated the healthcare utilization impacts of PSPS events, and while they are planned events, the shutoff of electricity to large populations may impact health similarly to other unplanned power outages. 



% para 2: pathways between exp --> outcome
Prior work suggests that power outages, in general, may result in elevated healthcare utilization via multiple pathways.\citep{casey2020power} When the power goes out, people cannot run temperature-controlling devices, air filtration systems, or use refrigerators and other electricity-dependent devices.\citep{casey2020power} This can reduce indoor air quality and cause stress that may precipitate adverse health outcomes. At particular risk are those reliant on DME, as back-up batteries generally last less than 12 hours.\citep{molinari2017s} Oxygen concentrators, a device commonly used by those with COPD, often have a battery life of 3-4 hours.\citep{molinari2017s}


Power outages have been associated with increased risk of healthcare utilization for injuries, hypothermia, hyperthermia, adverse cardiorespiratory outcomes, and mortality likely due to stress, lack of indoor thermal control, reduced emergency and other communication, interrupted medical care, and loss of power to electricity-dependent durable medical equipment (DME).\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate} Together, wildfire smoke and outages may increase the risk of adverse health outcomes by causing additional exposure when individuals cannot run air filtration systems or must leave their homes to access electricity, additional stress, and shutoff of DME such as oxygen concentrators. To our knowledge, there have been no epidemiological studies of this co-occurring exposure. Those living with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), who often rely on oxygen concentrators, may face disproportionate risks during power outages and exceptional risk when wildfire smoke is also present. 

PSPS events may also pose an amplified threat when wildfires still occur despite the shutoff, dually exposing individuals to the adverse impacts of losing power and to wildfire smoke. This may happen if a PSPS event is (1) used to prevent a wildfire from worsening, or (2) initiated to attempt to reduce already high wildfire risk, but a wildfire still occurs nearby. The hazard of co-occurring power outages and high concentrations of \PM may have synergistic health impacts that exceed the sum of their individual effects. 

This study aimed to assess the association between PSPS events, wildfire fine particulate matter (\PM) exposure, and their co-occurrence and adverse respiratory, COPD, cardiovascular, and psychiatric acute healthcare utilization in California from 2013--2019.
