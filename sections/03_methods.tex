\section{Methods}
\subsection{Study population}
In this zip code-level time-stratified case-crossover\citep{lumley2000bias} study in California, we used data on emergency department visits and hospital admissions among adults $\geq$ 20 years of age from 2013 -- 2019 from the California Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI), \citep{hcai_datasets} across 1,481 California zip codes where we had access to power outage data and at least one healthcare encounter occurred (97\% of zip codes) (figure \ref{zctas_included_map}). Based on findings in prior power outage and wildfire literature, we studied all-cause respiratory, COPD, cardiovascular, and psychiatric healthcare encounters.\citep{casey2020power, casey2024measuring} This study was approved by the California Health and Human Services Agency’s Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (project number 2021-116).

\subsection{Exposures and covariates}

\subsubsection{PSPS data}
We measured exposure to PSPS events from 2013--2022 using data from PSE Healthy Energy, which compiled historic PSPS events from three of California's major energy providers, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG\&E), San Diego Gas and Electric (SDG\&E), and Southern California Edison (SCE).\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS, cpuc_psps_2025} 

The PSPS dataset contains circuit-level information for each PSPS event across these three major utilities. A circuit is a network of electrical lines that deliver electricity from a power source to a defined service area of customers. A PSPS event may impact multiple circuits, so each circuit-event had a start time, end time, duration, and number of customers impacted.\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS, cpuc_psps_2025} Utilities may not de-energize an entire circuit, and circuits may be energized and de-energized multiple times throughout events. Because we lacked information on these two characteristics of outages, we assumed that the number of people affected were evenly spread across the circuit and that they were without power for the duration of the circuit-event. Given the small geographic area covered by circuits, exposure misclassification is likely minimal.

We overlaid these circuit-level data containing the number of customers affected\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS, cpuc_psps_2025} with high-resolution gridded estimates of population from the 2020 census in California\citep{depsky2022high} to estimate the number of people theoretically served by each circuit. We used this information to derive our exposure of interest, the total number of customers impacted each day of a PSPS event in each zip code. We examined both (1) same-day PSPS event exposure and (2) same-day + prior 3-days (\text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$, rolling average for days 0-3) PSPS event exposure. Based on tertiles of counts for each of these, we categorized the magnitude of PSPS events for both same-day and \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ exposure as mild (same-day: $\leq$ 61 without power, \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$: $\leq$ 45 without power), moderate (same-day: 61 $<$ \text{customers without power} $\geq$ 593, \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$: 45 $<$ \text{customers without power} $\geq$ 458), or severe (same-day: $>$ 593 customers without power, \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$: $>$ 458 customers without power) for each zip code-event. Most events exceeded a day, and the median duration was \medianduration{} hours. The PSPS tertiles for both exposure metrics were calculated at the zip code-event level to equally weight longer and shorter events and base the magnitude of severity on number of customers without power rather than duration. We also expected reduced exposure measurement error when moving from mild to moderate to severe PSPS events, as more people within a specified zip code were truly exposed during severe events. 

Because of highly variable population counts within zip codes, we also constructed a secondary metric meant to measure the relative magnitude of severity of the PSPS event. This metric was computed by multiplying the total number of customers without power by the proportion of customers affected in each zip code. We again created tertiles of exposure at the zip code-event level (\textbf{online data supplement}). Our primary analysis focuses on the absolute metric for the simplicity of interpretation. 

Finally, in a secondary analysis, we constructed a metric of PSPS duration. To do this, we calculated a \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}6}$ day PSPS exposure as the total number of hours without power by zip code in the week prior to the index or referent day.

\subsubsection{Wildfire smoke data}
We used daily wildfire \PM estimates at the zip code level.\citep{aguilera2023novel} The estimates were modeled using a set of machine learning models with multiple predictor variables, including air quality monitor data, aerosol optical depth, land cover, and meteorological conditions, to produce daily \PM concentrations.\citep{aguilera2023novel} Wildfire-specific \PM was then calculated by imputing non-wildfire-related \PM concentrations using a chained random forest algorithm and taking the difference between these values and modeled \PM concentrations. When comparing the observed \PM concentrations across all US Environmental Protection Agency Monitoring sites versus modeled \PM concentrations, Aguilera et al. reported $R^2 = 0.83$.  We used same-day wildfire \PM exposure for same-day models, and \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ averaged wildfire \PM exposure for \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ models. 

\subsubsection{Temperature data}
We linked gridded (4km grid) daily mean temperature data from gridMET extracted at the location of zip code population-weighted centroids to the index or referent day.\citep{abatzoglou2013development} 

\subsection{Outcomes}
Our outcome data relied on HCAI individual-level emergency department visits and unscheduled (unplanned/emergency) hospital admissions from 2013--2019, which included date of admission, associated diagnoses based on \textit{International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Edition} (\textit{ICD}-9) and Tenth Edition (\textit{ICD}-10) codes, hospital zip code, and patient’s residential zip code. When a patient's zip code was missing, we used the zip code of the hospital (n = \missingzipcases{} [ \percmissingzip{}\%] cases). 

Using primary diagnosis codes, we identified combined daily counts of emergency department visits and unscheduled hospital admissions for the following causes: all-cause respiratory, COPD, cardiovascular, and psychiatric (table \ref{tab:icd_codes}). Diagnostic codes outside the primary position were not used. We chose these cause groups based on previous research of power outages and health.\citep{casey2020power} We excluded scheduled hospitalizations (e.g., planned/elective admissions) and visits among those 19 years and younger. As such, our analysis includes emergency department encounters and acute hospital admissions, both typically originating in the emergency department.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
This study examined the association between PSPS events alone, same-day and \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ wildfire \PM alone, and their joint impacts on all-cause respiratory, COPD, cardiovascular, and psychiatric emergency department encounters and hospitalizations. We assessed same-day and \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ exposures. Patient index and referent days in this case-crossover study were linked to exposure data using zip code and date. The date of emergency department visit or hospital admission was considered the index day. We selected referent days for every index day by matching on zip code, day of week, month, and year. In other words, if an index encounter occurred on a Monday in January 2015, all other Mondays in January 2015 \textit{except} for the day of the hospital encounter, were considered referent days.

Case-crossover studies are commonly used to evaluate short-term exposures and acute outcomes, as they inherently control for time-invariant confounding factors; the time-stratified referent days control for day-of-week and seasonality.\citep{lumley2000bias} The primary analysis examined the effects of severe PSPS events using an absolute exposure metric and continuous wildfire \PM. Secondary analyses examined mild and moderate PSPS events, a relative PSPS exposure metric, and continuous wildfire \PM. Because PSPS events typically last more than 24 hours, we performed this analysis between both same-day and \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}3}$ PSPS and wildfire \PM exposure and healthcare encounters.

We examined the association between (1) a PSPS event alone, (2) wildfire \PM alone, (3) co-occurring PSPS event and wildfire \PM exposure and cause-specific emergency department visits and hospital admissions using a conditional logistic regression model for each health endpoint, accounting for the matched strata of case and controls. Each model included PSPS event exposure, wildfire \PM exposure modeled using a linear term per \SI[per-mode=symbol]{10}{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre}, an interaction term between PSPS event and wildfire \PM exposure to assess multiplicative interaction, and a natural cubic spline with three degrees of freedom on same-day maximum temperature (selected based on best model fit to account for the potential non-linear relationship between temperature and health outcomes) (\textbf{online data supplement}). We assessed co-exposure in two ways: (1) multiplicative scale interaction effect - the deviation from the expected combined effects of PSPS and wildfire \PM on a multiplicative scale; and (2) joint effect - total risk of both hazards when they co-occurred (\textbf{online data supplement}). In secondary analyses, we ran all models for three separate age groupings: (1) all ages over 20 years, (2) 20-64 years old, and (3) 65+ years old. 

To account for the possibility that longer PSPS events may have greater health impacts, in our secondary analysis, we ran a model with a linear term for total number of hours without power in the prior week (\text{lag}$_{0\text{-}6}$) as our main PSPS effect. We accordingly parameterized wildfire \PM as a \text{lag}$_{0\text{-}6}$ day average for consistency. All modeling methods were otherwise the same.

R version 4.4.2 and Python version 3.13.0 were used for all analyses. Code is available at https://github.com/laurenwilner/psps\_ca\_analysis.
