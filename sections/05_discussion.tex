\section{Discussion}
The frequency and severity of wildfires in California have been rapidly increasing.\citep{dennison2014large, li2021spatial, cal_fire_2022} Accordingly, there has been a rise in utilities initiating PSPS events in an effort to prevent causing or exacerbating wildfires.\citep{huang2023review} The decision to initiate a PSPS event reflects a balancing of the trade-off between risk of wildfire and risk of PSPS event, though there have been limited studies and regulations on the social and health risks of PSPS events.\citep{huang2023review} This study examined the potential health risks associated with PSPS events occurring alone and simultaneously with wildfire \PM exposure. 

Using a time-stratified case-crossover study we found an association between severe PSPS events and same-day all-cause respiratory and COPD-specific hospital and emergency department encounters in California from 2013 -- 2019. COPD, compared to all-cause respiratory encounters, consistently demonstrated a stronger relationship with all exposures. These associations were amplified in the presence of wildfire \PM exposure. We did not observe an association with less severe power outages, whether they co-occurred with wildfire \PM or not. We did not observe associations between PSPS events and wildfire \PM with cardiovascular or psychiatric hospital or emergency department encounters. 

Power outages may result in elevated healthcare utilization via multiple pathways.\citep{casey2020power} When the power goes out, people cannot run temperature-controlling devices, air filtration systems, or use refrigerators and other electricity-dependent devices. This can reduce environmental air quality and cause stress that may precipitate adverse health outcomes. At particular risk are those reliant on electricity-dependent medical equipment, as back-up batteries generally last less than 12 hours.\citep{molinari2017s} Oxygen concentrators, specifically, often have a battery life of 3-4 hours.\citep{molinari2017s}

Limited large-scale epidemiological studies have evaluated the relationship between power outages and health.\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate, mcbrien2025, molinari2017s, zhang2020power} Overall, this existing literature examining power outages and health has found moderately elevated health risks surrounding power outages, with the highest risk being among those dependent on electricity-dependent medical equipment.\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate, mcbrien2025, molinari2017s} In New York City, Zhang et al. examined power outages in New York City and found substantial increases in hospitalization rates for COPD,\citep{zhang2020power} and Dominianni et al. found an association between power outages and cardiovascular hospital encounters during the winter months.\citep{dominianni2018health} Two case studies identified increases in mental health care seeking following an outage, particularly due to increases in worry, anxiety, and stress surrounding an outage.\citep{xiao2019immediate, ibrahim2016erratic, moreno2019community} While our results cohere with the prior studies identifying associations between short-term power outage exposure and care-seeking for respiratory conditions, particularly COPD, we did not identify an association with either cardiovascular or psychiatric hospital or emergency department encounters. There are several potential reasons for this. For one, people report worry around PSPS events, however this worry may not severe enough to warrant psychiatric medical care in a hospital or emergency department setting. Further, cardiovascular-power outage associations have been mixed and potentially seasonal, which may explain the reasons for which our results diverge.  

Another source of discrepancy is that all prior studies focused on all power outages, while here we only evaluate PSPS events. PSPS events are a unique type of power outage. PSPS events are always planned, proactive cuts to power. Power utility companies are required to inform customers in advance as well as take precautions to mitigate the impacts of the outage.\citep{harding2025a} In California, PSPS events are meant to prevent wildfire ignition or stall the spread in the event that a wildfire has ignited. Co-occurrence of all-cause power outages with wildfires increased in California from 2018--2020 years.\citep{do2023spatiotemporal} It is likely part of this trend was driven by PSPS events. Despite their increasing prevalence, epidemiologic research on PSPS events remains sparse. Wong-Parodi found that individual experiences of PSPS events vary, but many people do experience some stress and worry related to the events, which may impact mental and physical health.\citep{WONGPARODI2022102495} Further, preliminary results from Harding et al. found that emergency department visits spiked surrounding PSPS events for cardiorespiratory, injury, and mental health related health conditions\citep{harding2024association} and motor vehicle crashes were also elevated surrounding these events.\citep{harding2025association} 

PSPS events pose particular danger to COPD patients, given their reliance on such medical devices, sensitivity to changes in the temperature or humidity around them, and frequent rate of comorbidities.\citep{casey2020power} Hess estimated that 5-10\% of those with COPD rely on long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT),\citep{hess2023oxygen} and Nishi et al. has estimated that almost 19\% of older adults, specifically, with COPD rely on supplemental oxygen.\citep{nishi2015oxygen} Approximately 80\% of oxygen prescriptions are provided by Medicare, indicating that this is particularly prevalent among older adults -- an already vulnerable population.\citep{suen2024national} Zhang et al. found that COPD hospitalization rates were significantly higher after power outages, a relationship that grew stronger as duration of the power outage increased.\citep{zhang2020power} Further, Lin et al. found that power outages significantly increased risk of respiratory disease, with the strongest effects being on COPD.\citep{lin2021immediate} Finally, Qu et al. found that a power outage was associated with elevated hospital admissions on lag days 0-2 following a power outage, citing the dependence on electricity for oxygen concentrators and increased air pollutants with decreased air filtration capacity as a some probable causes.\citep{qu2021power} Our results are consistent with these findings as we also show a strong association between PSPS events and COPD hospital and emergency department encounters. We do find, however, that while the association between PSPS events and COPD encounters is strong, the multiplicative interaction between wildfire \PM and PSPS event exposure for COPD is similar to that for all respiratory health endpoints. The combined effect, however, is much higher, which is due to the effects of power outages being elevated.

Previous epidemiologic studies have also examined the effects of wildfire \PM on health. In a meta-analysis of the health effects of wildfire \PM, Gould et al. found a 0.15\% increase in same-day all-cause mortality for a \SI[per-mode=symbol]{10}{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} increase in wildfire \PM.\citep{gould2024health} Gould et al. also found associations between wildfire \PM and respiratory hospitalizations, but found mixed evidence of associations with cardiovascular health endpoints.\citep{gould2024health} While Elser et al. found a relationship between wildfire \PM and psychiatric disorders such as dementia,\citep{elser2025wildfire} we did not have a large enough sample size to detect this relationship in isolation. Despite these links between wildfire \PM and health, to our knowledge no studies have looked at the effects of co-occurring power outages and wildfire \PM on health, nor the effects of PSPS-specific outages during wildfire smoke exposure.

As the rate of power outages co-occuring with other climate hazards is growing,\citep{do2023spatiotemporal, do2025spatiotemporal} we would expect PSPS events to grow at a similar or steeper rate. Further, given the nature of these cuts being a risk mitigation strategy, PSPS events may be more likely to occur surrounding a severe weather hazard. This study found that PSPS events are associated with elevated respiratory healthcare utilization, both when they occur simultaneously with wildfire \PM and when they occur in isolation. Given both the probability of them becoming more frequent and their association with adverse health outcomes, a comprehensive understanding of the public health implications of these events is essential to ensuring that these events continue to reduce, rather than elevate, risk to human health. 

\subsection{Limitations}
This study has several limitations. The exposure assessment leveraged power utility data at the utility circuit-level, which meant we did not have access to individual-level exposure data nor individual customer locations. In the absence of more granular information about where individuals were on the circuits, we assumed that all customers were evenly distributed across a circuit. This means that, particularly for larger circuits, we may have misclassified exposure if the distribution of people varied widely across the circuit. When we aggregated to the zip code, we may have misclassified exposed zip codes if individuals were not without power on the part of the circuit intersecting with a given zip code. Similarly, circuits may be partially energized and de-energized throughout a PSPS event. We did not have information on this, and therefore assumed that all of a circuit was out of power for the duration of a PSPS event. Again, this may have resulted in similar misclassification. 

Further, we had information on the customers affected, but not the customers served, on each circuit. To estimate a denominator for our analysis, we determined the number of individual people who would theoretically be served by a given circuit, using spatial overlays discussed above. However, our numerator reflected the number of households, or customers, served. Since the mean household size is $>$ 1 across California, we may have a slight dilution of our relative metric. 

Additionally, the average duration of these events spanned multiple days, and we examined only same-day exposure. Future studies should look at lagged effects of wildfire \PM and PSPS events. 

Third, we relied on linking exposure data using residential zip code. As such, we could not ensure that an individual was (1) at home when a PSPS event occurred and (2) lived in the part of the zip code that was exposed to a PSPS event. This is also a problem for wildfire \PM exposure ascertainment. 

Fourth, we are only capturing individuals who went to an emergency department or hospital. Individuals who seek healthcare may be systematically different than those who do not, and we are unable to determine the representativeness of our study population to all adults in California. Additionally, PSPS events may result in delayed care-seeking behavior and we only looked at same-day. 

We also group many disease endpoints together in our four categories (respiratory, COPD, psychiatric, and cardiovascular). This may be part of the reason that we found null results for psychiatric endpoints, while other studies did find some associations. For example, there is a wide discrepancy in care seeking behavior for some psychiatric endpoints, such as depression, compared to care seeking behavior for substance abuse. Our results for cardiovascular endpoints are consistent with some but not all of the power outage literature, and again it is possible that this is the result of grouping disparate cardiovascular endpoints together.

Finally, in an observational study we cannot be certain that all confounding has been accounted for. Our case-crossover design captures much of the confounding, but short-term time varying confounders may remain. We adjust for temperature to address the major trends that may confound this exposure-outcome relationship, but there may be residual confounding.

\subsection{Conclusion}
Since 2022, other states have also been implemented PSPS events, including Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Hawaii\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS} in response to growing wildfire risk. In order to mitigate wildfire risk without unintentionally imposing new, potentially higher, risks, it is essential that we understand both the independent and joint public health risks of these two exposures. While existing literature exists on wildfires and health as well as power outages and health, this study is provides an initial analysis of these co-occurring environmental hazards and highlights the elevated risk of respiratory hospital encounters during this dual exposure. Future studies should examine not only the effects of these independent hazards, but also the co-occurrence of environmental hazards, in order to adequately protect public health. While this is the first study to examine health impacts of wildfires and PSPS events, other studies may further examine this relationship with a particular emphasis on the role of heat.
