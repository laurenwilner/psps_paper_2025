% para 1: summarize findings
California's wildfire frequency and severity have increased rapidly.\citep{dennison2014large, li2021spatial, cal_fire_2022} In tandem, utilities have increased the number of PSPS events to reduce fire risk and spread.\citep{huang2023review} Using California data from 2013--2019 and a time-stratified case-crossover design, we found a positive association between severe PSPS events and same-day all-cause respiratory and COPD-specific, but not cardiovascular or psychiatric, hospital and emergency department encounters. The relationship between PSPS events and COPD encounters, compared to all-cause respiratory encounters, was consistently stronger. Wildfire \PM exposure was also only associated with same-day all-cause respiratory and COPD-specific hospital and emergency department encounters. Associations between PSPS events and respiratory endpoints were amplified in the presence of wildfire \PM exposure, especially for COPD. 

% para 2: pathways between exp --> outcome
Prior work suggests that power outages may result in elevated healthcare utilization via multiple pathways.\citep{casey2020power} When the power goes out, people cannot run temperature-controlling devices, air filtration systems, or use refrigerators and other electricity-dependent devices.\citep{casey2020power} This can reduce indoor air quality and cause stress that may precipitate adverse health outcomes. At particular risk are those reliant on DME, as back-up batteries generally last less than 12 hours.\citep{molinari2017s} Oxygen concentrators, a device commonly used by those with COPD, often have a battery life of 3-4 hours.\citep{molinari2017s}

% para 3: describe PO exposure measurement
The majority of studies on power outage and emergency department or hospital encounters have constructed binary power outage metrics. Specifically, these metrics leverage the portion of individuals without power in a specific geographic unit, generating a binary metric of power outage when a certain percentage of individuals lack power (e.g., 5\%).\citep{northrop2024power, do2025impact, lin2021immediate, lin2024joint, zhang2020power, deng2022independent} Other studies used a major power outage event, such as the 2003 blackout in New York, and assumed everyone in the affected geographic area to be exposed.\citep{anderson2011impacts, dominianni2018health} Skarha et al. used outage data at specific skilled nursing facilities, and assumed everyone who lived in an affected facility to be exposed.\citep{skarha2021association} 

% para 4: transition from exp measurement is diff to also our exp is different... summary of previous PSPS studies + health and again contextualize our study 
Our power outage exposure assessment deviated in several key ways from previous studies. First, prior studies focused on all power outages, while we only evaluated PSPS events. PSPS events are unique. They are always planned, proactive cuts to power. The decision to initiate a PSPS event reflects a balancing of the societal risks stemming from wildfires and the costs of PSPS event itself.\citep{huang2023review}  Power utility companies are required to inform customers in advance and take precautions to mitigate the impacts of the outage.\citep{harding2025a} Historically, PSPS events last longer than power outages. Accordingly, we assessed exposure at the daily zip code-level using two metrics by determining (1) the number of customers without power in a given zip code, and (2) the product of the number of customers without power and the proportion of customers without power as a measure of severity. Given the variability in zip code size, these absolute and relative metrics allowed us to measure exposure to power outages, specifically PSPS events, both in terms of the actual number of households affected as well as in terms of the portion of households affected (absolute) as well as the severity of the PSPS event (relative). Importantly, unlike previous studies, neither of our metrics were based on duration. This is because nearly all of the PSPS events in this study were longer than 8 hours, which is the cutoff generally used in previous studies of health.\citep{do2025spatiotemporal, northrop2024power} To explicitly avoid weighting outages differentially based on duration, and thus created our categorical metrics based on tertiles calculated at the event-zip code level. 

% para 5: summary of previous PO studies + health and contextualize our study 
Limited large-scale epidemiological studies have evaluated the relationship between any type of power outage and health.\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate, mcbrien2025, molinari2017s, zhang2020power}  Generally, the existing literature finds moderately elevated health risks following power outages, with some evidence that the highest risk is concentrated among those dependent on DME.\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate, mcbrien2025, molinari2017s, andresen2023understanding} Two studies in New York City identified elevated risk of all-cause mortality following the August 2003 black out\citep{anderson2011impacts, dominianni2018health} and numerous smaller scale outages.\citep{dominianni2018health} In the days following a power outage, studies in New York State and Florida have found increased risk of emergency department visits or hospitalizations for cardiovascular disease,\citep{deng2022independent} respiratory illness,\citep{dominianni2018health, lin2024joint, deng2022independent} COPD,\citep{zhang2020power, lin2024joint} food and water-borne disease,\citep{deng2022independent} injury,\citep{northrop2024power} renal disease,\citep{dominianni2018health} and carbon monoxide poisoning.\citep{northrop2024power} However, Lin et al. observed null associations between power outages and respiratory-, food and water-borne-, and injury-related hospitalizations.\citep{lin2021immediate} Additionally, both Lin et al. and Do et al. saw no association between power outages and cardiovascular hospitalizations in New York.\citep{lin2021immediate, do2025impact}  Participants in two qualitative studies reported increases in worry, anxiety, and stress following power outage.\citep{ibrahim2016erratic, moreno2019community} Despite characterizing exposure differently than most prior studies, our results for severe PSPS events and respiratory encounters align with the majority of the existing literature, as do our null results for cardiovascular disease, suggesting respiratory disease may be more sensitive to power outages than cardiovascular disease. However, we did not observe an association with less severe PSPS events. We also found no association with psychiatric hospital or emergency department encounters. While people report worry around PSPS events, their symptoms may not rise to a level warranting emergency psychiatric medical care. 

The population health literature around PSPS events remains sparse. Wong-Parodi found that individual experiences of PSPS events vary, but many people experience some stress and worry related to them, which may impact mental and physical health.\citep{WONGPARODI2022102495} Further, motor vehicle crashes remained largely unchanged during these events.\citep{harding2025a} Because many states now initiate PSPS events, future work must evaluate their impacts on health to inform the costs and benefits of these events. 

% para 6: wfs main effects and results and contextualize study within the context of this lit
Previous epidemiologic studies have also examined the effects of short-term wildfire \PM exposure on health. In a meta-analysis, Gould et al. reported a 0.25\% and 0.36\% increase in respiratory hospitalizations and ED visits, respectively, per \SI[per-mode=symbol]{}{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} increase in ambient wildfire \PM, but null results for short-term cardiovascular encounters.\citep{gould2024health} Several studies have also identified associations between short-term wildfire \PM exposure and COPD hospital encounters.\citep{sutherland2005wildfire, reid2019wildfire, wilgus2024clearing} Results are mixed for mental health-related healthcare utilization, with some evidence of same-day increases.\citep{cleland2022short, wettstein2024psychotropic, jung2025fine, chen2023emergency}  Our study is consistent with previous evidence of an association between wildfire \PM exposure and respiratory hospitalizations and emergency department visits. Our null results for cardiovascular and psychiatric healthcare utilization also align with prior literature. 

% para 7: multiplicative effects 
While both power outages and wildfire smoke are associated with adverse health outcomes, the effects of these two exposures together are understudied, despite a rise in their co-exposure.\citep{do2025spatiotemporal} Over a three-year study period from 2018 -- 2020, Do et al. found that among power outages co-occurring with any severe weather event, those co-occurring with wildfires increased in California, Oregon, and Washington.\citep{do2025spatiotemporal} This co-occurring exposure may have more than additive health effects. Several studies found that power outage co-occurring with extreme heat exacerbated adverse health effects.\citep{xu2025power, yamasaki2024heat, lin2011health} One study found that heat-related ambulance attendance nearly doubled when a power outage co-occurred.\citep{xu2025power} Other studies found heightened impacts of power outages that co-occurred with storms, wind events, and floods.\citep{lin2021immediate, lin2024joint} We found that the joint effects of PSPS events and wildfire \PM were stronger than the effects of either exposure alone on elevated respiratory healthcare utilization, particularly for COPD. This higher elevated combined effect for COPD was driven by a stronger relationship between PSPS events and COPD encounters than overall respiratory encounters. We also assessed multiplicative interaction between PSPS events and wildfire \PM exposure, finding a similar magnitude of association for COPD and overall respiratory encounters. These findings suggest that climate-driven hazards may be risk multipliers for power outage events.

% para 8: vulnerable pops + copd
Prior studies have found individuals reliant on electricity-dependent durable medical equipment, older adults, and those with pre-existing conditions are at higher risk following power outage exposure.\citep{molinari2017s, harding2024association, sheridan2021individual, deng2022independent, xu2025power, casey2020power} We observed the strongest associations between PSPS events and emergency department visits and hospitalizations for COPD. PSPS events likely pose particular danger to COPD patients, given their reliance on DME, sensitivity to temperature and humidity changes, and frequent comorbidities.\citep{casey2020power} Hess estimated that 5-10\% of those with COPD rely on long-term oxygen therapy,\citep{hess2023oxygen} and Nishi et al. estimated that almost 19\% of older adults with COPD rely on supplemental oxygen.\citep{nishi2015oxygen} In New York State, COPD hospitalization rates were significantly higher after power outages, and this relationship strengthened as power outage duration increased.\citep{zhang2020power} Also in New York State, Qu et al. found that power outage appeared to mediate 50\% to 65\% of the association between severe weather and COPD hospitalizations.\citep{qu2021power}

\subsection{Limitations}
This study had several limitations. The exposure assessment leveraged power utility data at the circuit level, meaning we did not have access to individual-level exposure data. In the absence of more granular information about individuals' locations on the circuits, we assumed an even customer distribution, which, especially for larger circuits, may have resulted in misclassified exposure. Similarly, circuits may have been partially energized and de-energized throughout a PSPS event. We lacked information on this, and therefore assumed that the entire circuit lacked power for the duration of the PSPS event. When we aggregated exposure to the zip code, some additional misclassification may have occurred if an individual did not live in the part of the zip code that was exposed to the PSPS event. Further, when estimating exposure data at the residential zip code-level, we could not ensure that an individual was at home when a PSPS event occurred. This limitation also applied to wildfire \PM exposure ascertainment. 

Second, the average duration of PSPS events spanned multiple days, and we examined only same-day exposure. Future studies should examine lagged effects of PSPS events and wildfire \PM exposure. 

Third, we only captured individuals 20 years and older who went to an emergency department or hospital. Individuals who seek healthcare may systematically differ from those who do not. Additionally, PSPS events may result in delayed care-seeking behavior, and while we only assessed same-day visits, future research should consider more extended periods. Because effects in children may differ, future studies should include their healthcare information. 

Fourth, we grouped many disease endpoints together into four categories (respiratory, COPD, psychiatric, and cardiovascular). This may explain our null results for overall psychiatric endpoints. There is a wide discrepancy in care-seeking behavior for some psychiatric endpoints, such as depression compared to substance abuse, and future studies may wish to disaggregate these endpoints. Our null results for cardiovascular disease endpoints may also have resulted from grouping disparate endpoints together.

Finally, in an observational study, unobserved confounding may remain. Our case-crossover design effectively handles time-invariant confounding, but short-term time-varying confounders may remain. We adjusted models for daily ambient temperature, but there may be residual confounding.

% para 9: conc
\subsection{Conclusion}
In the US, as the prevalence of wildfires and all power outages co-occurring with other climate hazards grows\citep{iglesias2022us, climatecentral2024weather} we expect the scope and frequency of PSPS events to similarly increase. Since 2022, states other than California have also implemented PSPS events, including Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Hawaii\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS} in response to growing wildfire risk. Further, because PSPS events are a risk mitigation strategy, they may more often occur during severe weather events. In order to mitigate wildfire risk without unintentionally imposing new health risks, an understanding of the public health risks posed by PSPS events alone and in concert with wildfire \PM is essential. This studyprovides the first analysis of these co-occurring hazards related to healthcare utilization, finding that PSPS events resulted in elevated odds of respiratory healthcare utilization, both when they occur in isolation and simultaneously with wildfire \PM. We also highlighted elevated risk among those with COPD. Given both the probability of PSPS events becoming more frequent and their potential association with adverse health outcomes, further research is necessary to characterize the public health implications of these events, in order to ensure that they reduce, rather than elevate, risks to human health.
