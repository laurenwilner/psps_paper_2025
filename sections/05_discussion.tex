\section{Discussion}

We used a time-stratified case-crossover study to examine the association between PSPS events and wildfire smoke \PM affected hospital and emergency room encounters among four different causes and cause groups. Drawing on PSPS and wildfire smoke \PM data from California from 2013 -- 2019, we used a conditional logistic regression and found an association between co-occurring severe PSPS outages and wildfire smoke \PM hospital and emergency department visits. This association was XX when we pulled out COPD, specifically, and performed the same analysis. We found null results when we examined cardiovascular and psychiatric causes, which we believe was due to small sample sizes. 

Power outages are typically associated with elevated healthcare utilization for a variety of reasons. We chose these cause groups based on previous research of power outages and health.\citep{casey2020power} Outages precipitate cardiorespiratory hospital and emergency department visits most notably due to patients' electricity-dependent medical devices failing without power. COPD patients are at particular risk given their reliance on such medical devices, sensitivity to changes in the temperature or humidity around them, and frequent rate of comorbidities.\citep{casey2020power} Lastly, we examined psychiatric visits as previous studies have shown an association between power outages and elevated anxiety and stress.\citep{casey2020power} Several case studies identified increases in mental health care seeking following an outage.\citep{xiao2019immediate, ibrahim2016erratic}

Overall, the epidemiologic literature examining power outages and health, while limited, has found moderately elevated health risks surrounding power outages, with the highest risk being among those dependent on electricity-dependent medical equipment.\citep{casey2020power, northrop2024power, lin2021immediate, mcbrien2025, molinari2017s} 

PSPS events, however, are a unique type of power outage. While power outages may or may not be planned, PSPS events are always planned, proactive cuts to power. Power utility companies are also required to inform customers in advance as well as take precautions to mitigate the impacts of the outage.\citep{harding2025a} Further, often, these cuts are risk mitigation strategies, which may mean that the PSPS events are more likely to occur surrounding a severe weather hazard. The rate of power outages co-occuring with other climate hazards is growing,\citep{do2023spatiotemporal, do2025spatiotemporal} and we would expect PSPS events to grow at a similar or steeper rate given their nature. While the limited research on power outages and health establish a moderate association, epidemiologic research on PSPS events, specifically, is sparse.\citep{harding2025association,xie2025, WONGPARODI2022102495, harding2024association} These studies show that individual experiences of PSPS events vary, but many people do experience some stress and worry related to the events, which may impact mental and physical health.\citep{WONGPARODI2022102495} Preliminary results from Harding et al. found that emergency department visits did spike surrounding PSPS events for cardiorespiratory, injury, and mental health related health conditions.\citep{harding2024association} 

Previous epidemiologic studies have also examined the effects of wildfire smoke \PM on health as well as the effects of power outages on health. In a meta-analysis of the health effects of wildfire smoke \PM, Gould et al. found a 0.15\% increase in same-day all-cause mortality for a \SI[per-mode=symbol]{10}{\micro\gram\per\cubic\metre} increase in wildfire smoke \PM.\citep{gould2024health} However, to our knowledge no studies have looked at the effects of co-occurring power outages and wildfire smoke \PM on health, nor the effects of PSPS-specific outages during wildfire smoke exposure.

As wildfires have been increasing in frequency and severity, PSPS events started as a policy adaptation in California where these wildfires are particularly prevalent. Since 2022, other states have since been implemented PSPS events, including Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Hawaii\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS} in response to this growing wildfire risk. In order to mitigate wildfire risk without unintentionally imposing new, potentially higher, risks, it is essential that we understand both the independent and joint public health risks of these two exposures. While existing literature exists on wildfires and health as well as power outages and health, this study is provides an initial analysis of these co-occurring environmental hazards and highlights the elevated risk of respiratory hospital encounters during this dual exposure. Future studies should examine not only the effects of these independent hazards, but also the co-occurrence of environmental hazards, in order to adequately protect public health. While this is the first study to examine health impacts of wildfires and PSPS events, other studies may further examine this relationship with a particular emphasis on the role of heat.

\subsection{Limitations}
This study has several limitations. The exposure assessment leveraged circuit-level data and we did not have access to individual-level exposure data nor individual customer locations. In the absence of more granular information about where individuals were on the circuits, we assumed that all customers were evenly distributed across a circuit. This means that, particularly for larger circuits, we may have misclassified exposure if the distribution of people varied widely across the circuit. When we aggregated to the zip code, we may have misclassified exposed zip codes if individuals were not without power on the part of the circuit intersecting with a given zip code. Similarly, circuits may be partially energized and de-energized throughout a PSPS event. We did not have information on this, and therefore assumed that all of a circuit was out of power for the duration of a PSPS event. Again, this may have resulted in similar misclassification. 

Further, we had information on the customers affected, but not the customers served, on each circuit. To estimate a denominator for our analysis, we determined the number of individual people who would theoretically be served by a given circuit, using spatial overlays discussed above. However, our numerator reflected the number of households, or customers, served. Since the mean household size is > 1 across California, we may have a slight dilution of our relative metric. 

Second, we relied on linking exposure data using residential zip code. As such, we could not ensure that an individual was (1) at home when a PSPS event occurred and (2) lived in the part of the zip code that was exposed to a PSPS event. 

Third, we are only capturing individuals who went to an emergency department or hospital. Individuals who seek healthcare may be systematically different than those who do not, and we are unable to determine the representativeness of our study population to all adults in California. 

Finally, in an observational study we cannot be certain that all confounding has been accounted for. Our case-crossover design captures much of the confounding, but longer term trends that we were unaware of may impact results.
