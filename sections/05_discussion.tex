\subsection{Limitations}
This study has several limitations. The exposure assessment leveraged circuit-level data and we did not have access to individual-level exposure data nor individual customer locations. In the absence of more granular information about where individuals were on the circuits, we assumed that all customers were evenly distributed across a circuit. This means that, particularly for larger circuits, we may have misclassified exposure if the distribution of people varied widely across the circuit. When we aggregated to the zip code, we may have misclassified exposed zip codes if individuals were not without power on the part of the circuit intersecting with a given zip code. Similarly, circuits may be partially energized and de-energized throughout a PSPS event. We did not have information on this, and therefore assumed that all of a circuit was out of power for the duration of a PSPS event. Again, this may have resulted in similar misclassification. 

Further, we had information on the customers affected, but not the customers served, on each circuit. To estimate a denominator for our analysis, we determined the number of individual people who would theoretically be served by a given circuit, using spatial overlays discussed above. However, our numerator reflected the number of households, or customers, served. Since the mean household size is > 1 across California, we may have a slight dilution of our relative metric. 

Second, we relied on linking exposure data using residential zip code. As such, we could not ensure that an individual was (1) at home when a PSPS event occurred and (2) lived in the part of the zip code that was exposed to a PSPS event. This is also a problem for wildfire \PM exposure ascertainment. 

Third, we are only capturing individuals who went to an emergency department or hospital. Individuals who seek healthcare may be systematically different than those who do not, and we are unable to determine the representativeness of our study population to all adults in California. Additionally, PSPS events may result in delayed care-seeking behavior and we only looked at same-day. 

Finally, in an observational study we cannot be certain that all confounding has been accounted for. Our case-crossover design captures much of the confounding, but longer term trends that we were unaware of may impact results.

\subsection{Conclusion}
Since 2022, other states have also been implemented PSPS events, including Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Hawaii\citep{PSEHealthyEnergy_PSPS} in response to growing wildfire risk. In order to mitigate wildfire risk without unintentionally imposing new, potentially higher, risks, it is essential that we understand both the independent and joint public health risks of these two exposures. While existing literature exists on wildfires and health as well as power outages and health, this study is provides an initial analysis of these co-occurring environmental hazards and highlights the elevated risk of respiratory hospital encounters during this dual exposure. Future studies should examine not only the effects of these independent hazards, but also the co-occurrence of environmental hazards, in order to adequately protect public health. While this is the first study to examine health impacts of wildfires and PSPS events, other studies may further examine this relationship with a particular emphasis on the role of heat.
